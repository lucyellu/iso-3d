Atlas of Temperature

From the coldest thing ever made to the hottest temperature physics can describe. On a logarithmic scale every long tick is ten times hotter, and absolute zero is not at the end of it: it lies infinitely far to the left, a limit you can approach forever and never reach. Or set any two side by side.

Temperatures are in kelvin, which starts at absolute zero, so “ten times hotter” means ten times the thermal energy per particle. Values are typical measured figures or, for the early universe, standard estimates; “~” marks approximate values. Italic entries are limits and scales set by constants rather than things. The band under the scale shows what ordinary matter becomes as it heats, from quantum gases to quark–gluon plasma; hover it for the thresholds, which are approximate. Past a break at each end sit the two temperatures no logarithmic scale can hold: absolute zero, infinitely far to the left, and negative kelvin temperatures, which are hotter than any positive one and so lie past infinity on the right. Their positions there are nominal.
